Who I Am

I'm an Astronomy PhD candidate and NASA FINESST Fellow at The Ohio State University (OSU), working with advisors Christopher Kochanek and Laura Lopez on estimating progenitor masses of core-collapse supernova remnants, like the Crab Pulsar.

I recently was awarded the Presidential Fellowship at OSU for the final year of my dissertation.

What I Study

I'm fascinated by single and binary massive star evolution, and by understanding how progenitor properties of core-collapse supernovae relate to their remnants — compact objects and nebulae — to unravel how massive stars evolve, explode, and shape their surroundings.

I'm also interested in the physics of explosive stellar death, and how binary interactions shape progenitor systems that leave behind compact objects which later merge to produce gravitational waves.

Research

Understanding Supernovae and Their Remnants

Primary Research: Supernova Progenitor Masses

My doctoral research focuses on estimating progenitor masses of core-collapse supernova remnants using two complementary techniques, supported by a NASA FINESST fellowship. Understanding progenitor mass matters for testing models of massive star evolution, understanding how heavy elements are created and distributed (nucleosynthesis), interpreting the chemical composition of galaxies, and improving our understanding of stellar explosions.

Case Study — The Crab Pulsar: the Crab Nebula and Crab Pulsar are among the best-studied supernova remnants. The progenitor star's mass is still debated, making it an excellent laboratory for testing and refining our methods.

Current Projects

Progenitor Mass Estimation (Ongoing PhD Dissertation) — analyzing supernova remnants in our Galaxy and the LMC to constrain progenitor masses using well known techniques refined during my research.

Broader Interests

Massive Star & Binary Evolution — how single and binary massive stars evolve, explode, and shape their surroundings.

Compact Object Formation — how binary interactions in progenitor systems influence the compact objects left behind, including systems that may later merge to produce gravitational waves.

Transient Phenomena — supernovae and tidal disruption events offer windows into extreme physics. I'm interested in their classification, physical mechanisms, multi-messenger observations, and population statistics.
